Something I recently read about is the discipline of teleology, which means deriving the understanding or importance of natural or human-made objects by understanding their final purpose or the goal they serve in our lives.

The teleological perspective was made popular by Plato and Aristotle, who believed that the universe was a perfect efficient machine (wherein all natural living beings, as well as human-made objects like knives or fire, existed efficiently to achieve their perfect final objective). For example, if we were to think about why a knife is the way it is and what makes it good, we could think about its evolutionary design and how its design and shape has changed over the years. Or we could think teleologically - what is the purpose of a knife? What must it do? It cuts. So a good knife must be a knife that cuts well. What is interesting is that this thinking also gave rise to certain branches of theology - a perfect universe designed to optimize for efficiency, must have been created by an intelligent creator.

Now I am being motivated to think about the purpose of artificial intelligence in our lives from the same teleological perspective. As far as I could understand up until now, we want AI to solve all problems existing in our humanity. We want AI to improve our human existence without ever exceeding our human existence.

Now how is that even possible? If we think etymologically, how was the term "artificial intelligence" derived? The part "artificial" in the word implies the synthesis of an intelligence that we would like to use to improve our lives. But growingly we never made the boundary in understanding how much of this intelligence would be similar or different from ours. When we created machines as tools for calculation, we never thought of calculators to completely remove the human ability or need to calculate. We never said that math careers would be obsolete, children won't be taught to calculate any more, or that calculation would never be needed to be done by humans. We simply understood the need for passing along long calculations to computers while we can coexist and practice the skill of calculation as we pleased.

It's because the task of synthesizing intelligence is so incredibly complex that it touches themes of morality, agency, capability, consciousness, and the intermixed domain of all of this combined (which is so much harder to understand, structure, and solve than just achieving success in a verifiable domain like math or coding or generation).

With all this in mind I find it hard to picture what the teleological purpose of AI should be. Furthermore if we ever reach a point where we're able to create intelligent beings and not just machines - beings that are capable of experiencing and performing the individual motivation to perform a task and the required capability to do that task, what will the teleological explanation look like then?

Will we think about what the final purpose of AI is for us? What makes it good for us or what makes it good for the AI itself? How then will we be able to prioritize what these machines should look like if we are integrating their designs to be beneficial and effective for us? Do we still get to be the so-called intelligent creators of artificially intelligent systems or do we perhaps sideline into mere observing states, losing control over the reality of our existence and the ones of our artificially intelligent superiors?
