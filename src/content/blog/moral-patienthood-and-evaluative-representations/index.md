I want to talk about 2 interesting ideas I read through 2 papers today. I'll start with Patrick Butlin's work by his MATS 9.0 Mentee Oscar Gilg, recently accepted into NeurIPS 2026: "Probing Persona Dependent Properties of LLMs". This paper tries to prove that LLMs operate according to fixed preferences, and these preferences can be tracked, stay robust to prefills and can be steered to change model's responses. This is one of the first work to show a statistical measure of evaluative representations within an LLM. The work reports revealed preferences and has a mechanism like so:
- The authors first create pairwise task choices based on samples from common QA datasets, math benchmarks and harmbench, to create a mix of harmful, utility and math-based tasks for LLMs to complete.
- The LLM is asked to choose between a pair. These pairwise choices are used to fit a scalar utility function. This is a statistical measure that is based on the Thurstonian model. 
- The authors then train a linear probe based on this utility function and it learns to predict the choice in pairwise prompts, by completing after the end-of-turn token for each choice. The vector learnt this way is called the "preference vector". Both instruct and pre-trained models are trained at residual stream activations for completion on these pairwise tasks.
- With this preference vector, the authors first test its performance on the default Assistant persona. They perform positive and negative steering to understand the effects of this preference vector and whether steering the probe in either direction affects the robustness of the model responses. 
- After this, the authors steer the probe in 6 additional personas. They find that multiple prompted personas share some aspects of the preference vector, but they are not shared agents masquerading with different masks.
The conclusions from this work felt powerful. The paper showed a definite way of capturing revealed preferences using a linear probe, steering along personas to show model response changes, and having the preference vector capture changes when the model's response/preference changed. This is an important indication of evaluative representations, and is as important of a result to AI safety as it is to philosophical fields like model welfare. This is because it sortof proves the existence of multiple personas steering the model's response in significant ways. For example: positive steering of the preference vector in the evil persona leads to more evil responses. This means that white-box safety methods like training deception probes or activation steering can show positive improvement when trained on 1 persona, but could very easily cause deceptive misalignment at deployment due to positive steering in the direction of a deceptive persona. While there are many limitations to this work that the authors admit, it was helpful to read about a deeply empirical approach to a philosophical concept in practice, and it taught me better on how to approach problems in this sphere.
## What question did I ask myself while reading, and can I answer it now?
I was excited to ask a lot of questions on multiple details of the experiment and wanted to understand in better detail how some aspects were designed. For example, my biggest qualm is with understanding whether personas can also be "revealed" rather than prompted by design or by a Persona Selection Model pipeline as designed by authors of the famous Persona Vectors work. I would like to understand the details of the Thurstonian model and see if maybe something similar can be used to create (ellicit?) an illustrious variety of personas to better understand the influence of preferences within each and how valuable it is to steer along either of these. I'm also wondering if personas can easily be prompted, then (as the authors correctly predict) most of the whitebox safety methods are rightfully voided in ability since we cannot guarantee the presence of a safe, HHH persona always for deployed models. I'd like to read the experimental setup in better detail to understand how they pick personas to steer against or for.
# Bottom-up approach to AI patienthood
The second article I read was a thoughtpiece by aforementioned Oscar Gilg, prescribing a new approach to identifying AI moral patienthood. So this current problem examines the current ability of AI systems to be moral patients: that is entities that are capable of exercising certain moral rights. Usually the current theories of ascribing moral patienthood to AI systems rely on picking specific moral theories of consciousness, deriving indicators of consciousness from this theory and testing models on whether they fit within this mould. This leads to 2 problems:
1. We make a lot of assumptions while deciding which theory of consciousness should be used to answer questions of moral patienthood, and these assumptions are not cleared by the theories we choose. I can explain by a simple example they list. David Chalmer ask the question: "Who do we talk to when we talk to an AI system?" Is it a model family? Is it a particular instance of the model family? Is it a particular deployed hardware of that model or is it a particular persona with that instance of the model? These are all assumptions you will have to make if you try to devise a theory and then try to fit the model to the properties of this theory.
2. Current theoretical moral welfare will not easily generalize to AI systems. There are a lot of different factors that we think about, like human consciousness. They can be sentience, agency, ability, or biological substrate, etc. We might rely on one or all of these factors in order to decide whether AI systems are capable of being accepted as moral patients.

The authors put forward this thought experiment (which might have been adapted from some other thinkpiece). They ask us to imagine an alien society where aliens live as robotic metallic bodies and they are able to live in a civilization similar to our own. They have developed culture and understanding. They are able to reproduce in a method that is alien to us, using their own mechanical bodies, and they're able to sustain families, hierarchies, social systems, and cultures.
Now if we were to take a particular baby of this society and there was a question of killing it to understand it better, and the mother of this child begs us not to, are we going to be morally wrong for "murdering" this baby when - by our current definitions of AI consciousness or AI moral patienthood - they might not qualify to be moral patients? Not granting them moral patienthood by our current definitions does not mean that killing this baby would be right in this example. 

Therefore the call is to think of empirical-first approaches to model welfare that build on philosophical claims, or better yet, conduct empirical experiments and then put them under a philosophical lens to answer the questions we wish to ask, and formulate our theories along the way. I found this immediately agreeable because this takes away the usual problem of no universal agreement of an understanding of human consciousness or human moral patienthood, and it frees us to think of AI systems as non-descendants of our own consciousness, and to solve their problems independently, while also relying on instruments we have with us.
