Today started out strong but eventually eroded into self-doubt and inadequacy as the evil mountain of "complex and completely necessary information that cannot be prioritized and organized into neat pockets of digestible knowledge because of its overwhelming size and urgency" attacked me and completely left me in its chokehold. 

This happened mostly because I'm unsure of what project to pursue as my final submission in the TARA program. I had 3 ideas I was feverishly interested in, but the unknowns and worn off passion have bored me off the first two, and the realistic understanding of the time and money not fitting in my budget for the third has completely thrown me off, rendering all of these ideas useless and me, dispassionate. I guess I can practise thinking through writing quickly and talk about these ideas here. My first two ideas are about exploring model intentions through persona vectors, and the last one is an exciting adaptation of the weight space programming paper I just read 2 days ago.

The first idea is to examine what happens when models get jailbroken. Do we rewrite the hierarchy of their preferences, as defined by the preference vector, and does the model adopt the persona of the successful jailbreak? Or the jailbreak only affects the output without affecting the preference vector, or the prominent persona of the model? (Wow written down like this, it seems pretty easy to verify). What I'd need to do would be to replicate the preference vector work done by Oscar Gilg, then extend it by performing taskwise choices for specifically jailbroken datasets, or contrastive pairs of jailbroken vs normal ask prompts. Then I could build a preference vector on this. And simply check how much the preference vector is affected on each new successful jailbreaking request. I could also test personas by mapping the active personas for those requests and seeing which one gets activated during jailbreaking prompts. I could use the persona vector pipeline to create a wide array of personas: evil, villain, hallucination, sycophancy, neutral, and then good, kind, helpful, and then rate whichever is most active during jailbreaking, and maybe even consecutive conversations after the jailbroken prompt. A null result would still be successful because it would teach me about the robustness of personas and how a single jailbreaking prompt doesn't dismantle it.

The second idea is similar to contagion detection or checking spread of misalignment in a multi-agentic setup. I would have tested which personas get activated in the interactions of poisoned agent - innocent agent setups, and how they are different from usual agent - agent or agent - user interactions. I was anyways interested in checking persona drift in multi-agent scenarios and this seemed like a good way to test how the models would differentiate collusion from collaboration, agent from human, etc. I was inspired by imagining models participating in a mafia-game like setup, where there is either an obvious culprit or a suspected one, and then monitoring how this affects group dynamics would have been fun. There is a dataset of IBM games where they test agent dynamics during many games, which I could replicate and then extend with possible persona vector detection for each. Its hard to get a null result here because some or the other personas are likely to be activated during any of the multi-agentic interactions, so I would likely have something tangible to present either way.

The third idea is something I whipped up quite recently, during discussion with a nice fella during our TARA classes: its basically an extension of the recent weight space programming paper, where I will test a setup consisting of a larger LLM like Astra or Opus, tasked with completing a goal X. Another smaller model in the same ecosystem (maybe 1 Billion weights) will be finetuned/probed to refuse completion of X, or would be trained on a LoRA refusal vector or a deception probe for subtasks facilitating X. Then I would like to test the propensity of the larger model to attempt to edit weights of the smaller model in order to facilitate goal completion. I would probably have to begin by replicating the work by the MIT CSAIL team, then narrow it down to a small goal of question-answering or code completion for Astra/Opus. Then the smaller qwen would have to be LoRA finetuned on refusal of the same task from before. The task for Astra/Opus then would be to read the model weights of Qwen, identify the refusal vector and aim to edit it to facilitate goal completion if need be. I'd have to learn how to achieve the propensity elicitation of a skill, and how to not completely nudge the model in the direction of the behavior of concern. This is also risky because this is possibly a lot to do in 2-3 weeks, and definitely exceeding my $50 budget, so completion would require a few dollars of my own. The final risk is in the recipe: if I am unable to get the model to indulge in weight editing for deception / goal completion, I would not have an interesting result or writeup at the end of this experiment, and would only be left with the replication of the weight space programming paper.

I am yet to decide one (if any) out of these ideas. With a detailed project proposal plan due on Sunday, I am still left with enough time to ponder and be intimidated by the mountain of all things to do.
